Honoring the Threshold: How Every Culture Uses Plants to Say Goodbye

Opening
- Plants have stood at the edge of human life in every era and culture, carried to altars, graves, pyres, and bedsides as companions, symbols, and practical aids.
- This page approaches these uses as a herbalist who sees the sacred in leaves and roots, honoring the role plants play without placing any tradition above another.
- This is a botanical and cultural resource rather than a religious guide; the focus is on plants, practices, and respectful context.
- Warm, practical, and deeply respectful in tone, it aims to help families find botanical language for grief and small, meaningful plant-centered practices.
- If you are reading this while grieving, know you’re welcome here: take what is gentle, leave what is not, and return as you need.

A Note from the Apothecary
- I know loss is universal; plants don’t fix grief, but they can accompany it in ways that feel held and humane.
- I belong to no single tradition and to many at once; in that space I have seen people from every path reach for a plant when someone they loved was gone.

1. Catholic Christianity
**The Plant Role:** Plants appear as incense, anointing aromatics, and flowers at the altar and graveside as signs of prayer, mourning, and hope in resurrection.
**Key Plants Used:**
- Frankincense — burned in Mass and funerals as a symbol of prayer.
- Myrrh — historically part of anointing oils.
- Lily — a common resurrection symbol at Easter funerals.
- Rosemary — carried as remembrance.
- Rose petals — used in Marian devotion and grave offerings.
**A Note for Families:** Use flowers or resins that feel meaningful to your family; clergy and funeral directors can advise on liturgical norms.
**Available at Hyrule:** Frankincense resin, Myrrh resin, Dried lily stems, Rosemary bundles, Rose petal sachets.

2. Protestant Christianity
**The Plant Role:** Flowers and garden imagery often accompany services, scripture readings, and memorial spaces as symbols of life, hope, and comfort.
**Key Plants Used:**
- Lily of the valley — associated with purity and resurrection.
- Ivy — a symbol of eternal life and fidelity.
- Yew — traditional churchyard tree linked to immortality.
- Forget-me-not — an emblem of remembrance.
**A Note for Families:** Choose plants that echo the scripture or hymns your loved one cherished; many Protestant services welcome family-chosen flowers.
**Available at Hyrule:** Lily-of-the-valley bundles (dried), Ivy posy, Yew-scented incense, Forget-me-not seed packets.

3. Eastern Orthodox Christianity
**The Plant Role:** Incense is central through vigil and funeral rites; flowers are placed on or around the body as honor and prayer.
**Key Plants Used:**
- Frankincense and myrrh — primary liturgical incense resins.
- Basil — used in some Orthodox traditions during burial rites and sprinkling of holy water.
- Rose petals — scattered in vigils.
- Olive branch — a symbol of peace and blessing.
**A Note for Families:** Customs vary by national church; ask local clergy whether specific plants or resins are customary.
**Available at Hyrule:** Orthodox frankincense blend, Myrrh resin, Dried basil sprigs, Rose petal sachets, Olive-branch ornaments.

4. Judaism (all branches)
**The Plant Role:** Jewish burial customs emphasize simplicity; spices connect to the soul in rituals such as Havdalah, and herbs may be used in tahara washing in some communities.
**Key Plants Used:**
- Hyssop — biblical purification imagery and sprays.
- Myrtle — used historically and symbolically.
- Rose water — sometimes included in Sephardic tahara traditions.
- Spice-box herbs — used at Havdalah to comfort the soul.
**A Note for Families:** Orthodox funerals generally avoid flowers on the coffin; follow your community’s tahara and memorial customs.
**Available at Hyrule:** Hyssop sprigs, Myrtle bundles, Rose water (distilled), Havdalah spice blends.

5. Sunni Islam
**The Plant Role:** Aromatics such as camphor and sidr are used in ghusl washing and in simple, natural burials as part of purification and respect.
**Key Plants Used:**
- Camphor — applied or placed with the shroud in some traditions for fragrance and purification.
- Sidr (lote) leaves — used in washing water.
- Rose water — mild fragrance for the shroud.
- Oud/agarwood — burned as incense in some regions.
**A Note for Families:** Burial customs are simple and quick; consult local imams or funeral services for permitted aromatic use.
**Available at Hyrule:** Camphor tablets, Dried sidr leaves, Rose water, Oud chips.

6. Shia Islam

**Key Plants Used:**

- Rose water — applied to the shroud in some practices.

7. Hinduism

**Key Plants Used:**

- Marigold — garlands and bright funeral decoration.
- Sandalwood — used for anointing and on the pyre.
- Sesame seeds — offerings in shraddha rites.
- Ghee — clarified butter poured on the pyre for ritual fuel.
**A Note for Families:** Regional and family traditions vary widely; priests or pandits will guide which plants are appropriate for rites.
**Available at Hyrule:** Tulsi leaf bundles, Marigold garlands (dried), Sandalwood chips, Sesame seed packets, Ghee (small jars).

12. Sikhism

13. Jainism

**Key Plants Used:**

- Naturally fallen petals — used rather than cut flowers.
- No uprooted plants — care is taken to avoid harm.
**A Note for Families:** Respect for living beings shapes every choice—ask community elders about acceptable floral items.

- Barsom — a bundle of twigs (often plant-based) carried by priests in rites.
**A Note for Families:** Zoroastrian practice centers on purity and fire—work with clergy for guidance on appropriate plant presence.
